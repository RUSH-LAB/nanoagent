Research is not about following a rigid plan. It is about exploring, finding signals, and turning those signals into ideas worth sharing. Here is what I have learned about doing research well.

<!-- truncate -->

## Research Taste

Think from first principles and find the most fundamental essence.

- Finish all your papers a month before your deadline.
- Need perfection in your paper writing, websites, and presentations. Have high quality standards.
- Question everything. Have a skeptical mind.
- You are your own genius. Dare to do something different.
- Research should be bottom-up, not planned. It involves exploring, finding signals, and turning those signals into ideas worth sharing.
- Your research idea's code should be as simple as possible. If your idea can be done in 300 lines instead of 3000, the 300-line idea is typically better.

## Find the Right Problem

How to define a problem is the most important capability in research. You need to develop a sense for what is both interesting and important. Interesting problems keep you motivated. Important problems ensure your work matters. The best research sits at the intersection of the two.

## The Hard Truth

Nobody cares about average work. In research, people only remember your best work — the work that everybody knows. This is not a reason to be paralyzed, but it is a reason to be ambitious. Aim for work that changes how people think about a problem, not work that merely adds another row to a table.

The competition is real. But the best way to stand out is not to compete on the same problems as everyone else — it is to find better problems.

## Build Strong Engineering Skills

Ideas alone are not enough. You need the engineering skills to prototype quickly, reproduce baselines, and iterate on experiments. The faster you can turn an idea into a working system, the more ideas you can explore, and the better your intuition becomes.

## Sharpen Your Tools First

A craftsman cannot do great work without great tools. Invest in solid infrastructure, a clean codebase, and the right tooling. These are not distractions from research — they are what make fast, reliable experimentation possible.

## Read Papers Strategically

Reading papers is essential, but not all parts of a paper deserve equal attention. Learn to distinguish what is important from what is not. Focus on the core contributions, the experimental setup, and the results that reveal something surprising. Skim the rest.

## The Research Cycle

A realistic research cycle looks something like this:

**Month 1–2: Explore and hack.** Know your general direction, but stay loose. Reproduce baselines and try extensions. The goal is not to have a finished idea — it is to find a signal. What works? What does not work? Let the experiments guide you.

**Month 3–5: Make the idea work.** Once you have a signal, commit to it. Refine the approach, run thorough experiments, and build a convincing story around your results.

**Month 6: Publish.** Write it up and put it out into the world.

## Predict Before You Run

Before every experiment, make a prediction about what the results will look like. This forces you to clarify your assumptions and sharpen your understanding. When the results match your prediction, your mental model is confirmed. When they don't, that gap between expectation and reality is where the most valuable signals live. Every experiment should teach you something — but only if you know what you expected in the first place.

## Signals Over Ideas

The most underrated part of research is the exploration phase. When you explore a field and pay close attention, you start to notice signals — patterns in what works and what does not. These signals are where real ideas come from.

Counterintuitively, signals about what does *not* work are often more valuable. They reveal gaps, broken assumptions, and overlooked directions. They point you toward problems that others have missed.

## Your Ceiling Is Your Baseline

The upper bound of your research is determined by your baseline. Push your baseline as high as it can possibly go, and only then try to beat it. If you can surpass an already-strong baseline, that is groundbreaking work. If your baseline is weak, even a big improvement might not mean much.

## What Bad Research Looks Like

If you define an idea at the start and the final paper looks exactly like that initial idea, then the idea was probably boring. Good research evolves. The best papers end up somewhere the author did not fully expect when they started. If your results never surprise you, you are not exploring enough.
